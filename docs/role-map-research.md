# Role map research

Candidate duties for Tend's role map, drawn from management literature and
current practice, filtered for a crossed matrix role.

This is input for a conversation, not a finished map. Read the shortlist at the
bottom first; the long list above it is the reasoning.

## How to read this, and what it is not

**These are summaries and secondary sources, not the books.** The Manager's Path
is the one to check against directly if any of this matters enough to verify. Everything here came from chapter
summaries, reading notes and practitioner articles, which means a specific
frequency or phrasing attributed to an author may be the summariser's rather
than the author's. Where a number appears below it is marked with where it came
from.

Two things follow. Anything that survives into the real role map should be
checked against the actual text if it matters. And the value here is less the
citations than the filter: which of these duties apply to a crossed matrix role,
where you lead people you do not manage and manage people you cannot see.

**Sources used:** [The Manager's Path summary (Runn)](https://www.runn.io/blog/the-managers-path-summary),

## The filter

Tend is built for a **crossed** reporting structure, and that is what makes most
of the standard advice need rewriting. Crossed means:

- Some people you lead day to day, you do not manage.
- Some people you manage sit in other teams, and you no longer observe their
  work.
- Some of the people you work most closely with are peers with no authority
  either way.
- You are accountable for projects you are not in the daily work of.

Almost all management writing assumes "my team" and "my reports" are the same
set of people. When they are not, two things move to the top that the books
barely cover: duties about **information you do not have**, and duties about
**influence you have no authority behind**.

Hiring, firing, performance improvement plans, managing managers and
organisational design are all well covered in the literature and all out of
scope here - they assume authority this structure does not grant.

## Candidate duties

### 1. A recurring 1-1 with everyone you manage

**Source:** universal. Fournier treats it as the base unit of the job; Hogan's
first-level managers run "hour-long 1:1s with direct reports weekly"; Grove ties
frequency to how experienced the person is at the work rather than a fixed
schedule.

**In practice:** the literature pushes for weekly on a small team. Fortnightly is
a defensible trade when the roster also includes people in other teams, since
the scarce resource is attention rather than calendar slots.

**How Tend knows:** a Nib note about that person. Derived, no confirmation.

### 2. A real information channel on the people you cannot see

**Source:** Hogan on skip-levels (she settled on half an hour every eight weeks
once her org passed 30 people). Fournier's chapters on managing at a distance.
The general principle in matrix literature: a solid-line manager who has lost
first-hand observation has lost the input the role runs on.

**Why it matters here:** the biggest structural gap, and the one nothing else covers.
Being the formal manager for people whose work you no longer see makes level-
setting either second-hand or guesswork. Periodic surveys help, but a quarterly
snapshot is not a channel; a short standing exchange with the other teams' leads
is.

**How Tend knows:** a logged exchange about that person from someone other than
the person. Distinct from a 1-1, so a 1-1 does not accidentally satisfy it.

**Suggested cadence:** monthly per remote report.

### 3. Feedback close to the event

**Source:** Fournier is direct that saving feedback for review season makes it
useless and turns help into a verdict. Hogan's model is Observation + Impact +
Request, replacing judgement with something actionable. Grove: criticism in
private, praise in public.

**Why it matters here:** unprompted feedback leaves no trace anywhere, so it is
the duty most likely to be quietly skipped for months without anyone noticing -
including you. It is the classic new-manager gap.

**How Tend knows:** a feedback entry against a person, which does need a
deliberate log. Weakest evidence path in this list, so the alert should be about
absence over weeks rather than a per-instance tick.

**Suggested cadence:** no schedule; flag a person who has had nothing in four
weeks.

### 4. A running record of what each person actually did

**Source:** the strongest evidence base of anything here. Reported figures:
around two thirds of managers weight reviews toward the most recent two to three
months, and close to half of employees feel their review missed most of the
year. The recommended countermeasure is consistently a per-person log updated
weekly, plus a self-assessment from the person covering the whole period.

**Why it matters here:** collecting other people's impressions to avoid relying
on your own is the same instinct one step later than this. A running record
feeds level-setting directly, and for people in other teams it may be the only
record that exists at all.

**How Tend knows:** entries against a person over time. Partly derived from Nib
notes, partly deliberate.

**Suggested cadence:** flag a person with no entry in six weeks.

### 5. Say how far you have delegated, out loud

**Source:** Grove, and the cleanest idea in this whole document. Task-relevant
maturity: how closely you monitor depends on how experienced the person is at
*this specific task*, not on how good they are generally. Low maturity gets
precise instruction; medium gets two-way communication and support; high gets
objectives and minimal involvement. And the line that matters:
**the presence or absence of monitoring is what separates delegating from
abdicating.**

**Why it matters here:** this is the missing half of any player-coach split. The
model says step back; it does not say how far in each case. Grove's framing
turns a vague intention into a decision per piece of work, and names the failure
mode as abdication rather than as trust.

**How Tend knows:** a level recorded per project or workstream, reviewed
monthly. Deliberate, but it is a handful of rows, not a habit.

### 6. Watch for silence, not just for slippage

**Source:** Fournier's warning signs, plus a consistent picture across current
practitioner writing on disengagement. The concrete signals: the senior who used
to push back in design reviews stops; retros end early with polite observations
and no tension; standups become mechanical; reviews come back "LGTM" instead of
substantive; PR descriptions get shorter; someone stops complaining about the
thing they always complained about. One framing worth keeping: a quiet team is
usually not a lazy team, it is a team that has learned speaking up changes
nothing.

**Why it matters here:** delivery is a lagging indicator, and in a crossed
structure you are furthest from the daily signal on exactly the people you are
formally responsible for. This is the duty that most needs to be a question the
tool asks, because the answer is usually no and the one time it is yes is worth
the whole exercise.

**How Tend knows:** it cannot. This is a prompted monthly question set, six
questions on one screen. The honest exception to "never ask for what can be
derived".

### 7. Sideways: the other three leads

**Source:** thin in the books, strong in practice writing on matrix and
cross-functional work. The recurring finding is that influence without authority
runs on trust and demonstrated reliability rather than on the coordinating role
itself, and that peer relationships are the direction leaders neglect first
because nothing forces them.

**Why it matters here:** coordination across peers has no authority behind it, so
it runs entirely on goodwill and has nothing in a calendar protecting it. Every
other duty in this list is anchored to something - a report, a project, a review
cycle. This one is anchored to nothing, which is exactly why it is the first to
go when a week gets busy.

**How Tend knows:** a logged exchange with a lead outside a scheduled meeting.

**Suggested cadence:** weekly, stretched to fortnightly under a focus.

### 8. Stay technical without writing the code

**Source:** LeadDev and adjacent writing converge on the same answer: credibility
does not come from output, it comes from understanding what is being built and
why. Practical forms are reading RFCs and design docs, asking senior engineers
for walkthroughs, pulling the repo and reading around a change. On a team of six
to ten there are enough engineers to do the work without the manager in it.

**Why it matters here:** directly relevant to a player-coach split, and the
thing that quietly erodes when the leadership half wins. But it resists
measurement, and a duty that can only be self-reported becomes a guilt counter.

**Recommendation:** not a tracked duty. Fold it into the delegation levels in #5,
where "reviewed" already implies having read the thing.

### 9. Written development direction per person

**Source:** Fournier separates the recurring career conversation from a written
direction that can be revisited. Zhuo's People bucket makes the manager
accountable for whether each person is set up to succeed and growing.

**Why it matters here:** a recurring career slot in a 1-1 covers the
conversation. The written half is what makes it checkable a year later, and what
makes a level conversation about evidence rather than impressions.

**Recommendation:** strong candidate, but it overlaps #4 heavily. Suggest
deferring it and revisiting once the running record exists, rather than tracking
both.

### 10. Protected thinking time

**Source:** Grove's leverage argument implies it. Practitioner writing on
player-coach roles is blunt that the reactive half eats the rest.

**Recommendation:** real, but it is a calendar rule rather than a duty that can
drift measurably. Belongs in the Focus view's week shape, which is where the mock
already puts it.

### 11. Purpose and process for the team

**Source:** Zhuo's framing that a manager's job is purpose, people and process,
and that the multiplier comes from those rather than from doing the work.

**Recommendation:** true and too abstract to track. A duty called "does the team
know what success looks like" produces a monthly guilt prompt with no action
attached. Leave it out; it shows up concretely through #6 anyway.

## Recommended shortlist

Seven, of which two already exist. Deliberately smaller than the candidate list:
a role map with fifteen entries is a role map nobody reads.

| # | Duty | Status | Cadence | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1-1 with everyone you manage | has | 2 weeks per person | derived from Nib |
| 2 | Information channel on people you cannot see | **new** | monthly per remote report | logged exchange |
| 3 | Feedback close to the event | **new** | flag at 4 weeks of nothing | logged |
| 4 | Running record of what each person did | **new** | flag at 6 weeks of nothing | part derived |
| 5 | Stated delegation level per workstream | **new** | reviewed monthly | recorded per row |
| 6 | Watch for silence | **new** | prompted monthly, 6 questions | asked, not derived |
| 7 | Sideways with the other leads | **new** | weekly | logged exchange |
| — | Producer and peer feedback rounds | has | quarterly + project end | survey log |
| — | Know where each project stands | has | 14-day threshold | derived |

Two of these seven are guarded, meaning a focus never mutes them: #1, and #2
because it is the blind spot everything else widens.

## What to check before this becomes the real map

- **The Manager's Path attributions.** Duties 1, 3, 6 and 9 are attributed to it
  from summaries rather than the text. Worth confirming the framing before the
  role map quotes it back at anyone.
- **Whether #3 and #4 are one duty or two.** They share an evidence path and both
  fail the same way. Merging them into "keep a record and act on it while it is
  fresh" may be honest; keeping them apart may be clearer. This is a judgement
  call, not a research question.
- **Whether #6 should be six questions or three.** Six is one screen but it is
  also a monthly interruption with an expected answer of no. Three would get
  answered.
- **Whether the delegation levels in #5 attach to projects, to people, or to
  both.** For a project it is per workstream. For coaching someone directly it
  is per person. That may be two different things wearing one name.

## Not researched

- Hiring, onboarding, performance improvement plans, terminations. Out of scope
  for a first-level role in a crossed structure.
- Managing managers. A different level, and a different book.
- Anything about compensation. Whether it is even in scope varies by org.
